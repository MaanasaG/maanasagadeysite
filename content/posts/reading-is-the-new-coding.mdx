### The problem

## PRs are up. Specs are up. Your eyeballs did not get a 10x.

PRs are up. Specs are up. Every meeting spawns a transcript, a summary, and a Slack thread arguing about the summary. You still read at ~250 words per minute. You still need 7 hours of sleep. The math is not on your side.

Production scaled exponentially. Your attention scaled by zero. The dashboards say green. You are drowning in green.

### The trap

## You&rsquo;re trying to read it all. Adorable.

The &ldquo;good engineer reads everything&rdquo; instinct was already wobbly in 2019. In 2026 it&rsquo;s a math error. A 40-person team running agents ships maybe 200 PRs a week. At 5 minutes each that&rsquo;s a 16-hour reading job. You have not written code. You have not slept. You have read PRs.

Open any engineering Slack right now. The 👀 reacts on PRs outnumber the ✅ ten to one. People aren&rsquo;t reviewing. They&rsquo;re performing reviewing.

The ones thriving are reading less, choosing harder, and somehow look better-rested than you. Their over-informed colleagues are busier, twitchier, and shipping less. Reading everything used to mean you cared. Now it means you haven&rsquo;t picked.

### The shift

## Producing got cheap. Reading is where the leverage is hiding.

Senior engineering used to be ~30% reading, ~70% producing. Flip it. Producing got cheap. Reading is where the leverage is hiding.

Triage is the new skill. It looks like:

<ul>
  <li>Skim, read, skip — and knowing which inside 10 seconds.</li>
  <li>Saying &ldquo;I&rsquo;m not reviewing that&rdquo; without the apology voice.</li>
  <li>Trusting summaries 90% of the time and knowing the 10% when you can&rsquo;t.</li>
</ul>

You will feel less informed. You will be less informed. The only question that matters: less informed about the right things, or the wrong ones?

### The toolkit

## Six moves, all measurable

<ol className="metric-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Default to no-read.</strong> Opt in, not out. If it doesn&rsquo;t @ you, name you, or block you, skip it. The things that actually need you will find you twice.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Tier your sources.</strong> Tier 1 (read fully): cap at 5. Tier 2 (skim weekly): cap at 15. Tier 3 (search on demand): everything else. If your Tier 1 has 40 things, it&rsquo;s a list.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Make the agent read first.</strong> 30-page RFC → 1-page summary + 3 real questions. Target 10x compression before you spend a single neuron. Summaries are cheap. Your judgment isn&rsquo;t.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Cap your reviews.</strong> Pick a number and post it somewhere visible. Suggested floor: 3 serious PR reviews, 2 design docs, 1 postmortem per week. Past the cap you decline with a reason. Capacity you don&rsquo;t declare gets eaten by people who needed you to say yes.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Write less, link more.</strong> Every doc you write is a doc someone else has to read. Before writing: search. If it exists, link. If you must write, target half the length you had in mind. Half again if it&rsquo;s a status update.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Defend 90 minutes a week of deep reading.</strong> One thing. Not seven things. On the calendar. Labeled. Decline conflicts. This used to happen by default when production was slow. Production is no longer slow.
  </li>
</ol>

### One last thing

## The new senior skill is not reading things

The engineers who can say &ldquo;haven&rsquo;t read it, not going to&rdquo; — and then absolutely cook on the things they did read — are operating on the right model.

Three buckets, from today:

<ul>
  <li><strong>Know deeply:</strong> ~5 things.</li>
  <li><strong>Know via summary:</strong> ~50 things.</li>
  <li><strong>Will never know:</strong> everything else. By volume, this is most of what is happening at your company. That&rsquo;s not a failure of diligence. It&rsquo;s the math, and the math is not going to apologize.</li>
</ul>

Read less. Read better. Pick.
